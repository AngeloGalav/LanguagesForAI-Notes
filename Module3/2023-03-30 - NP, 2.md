## The Cook-Levin Theorem (Conjuctive Normal Form - CNF)
A propositional formula $F$ is said to be in __conjunctive normal form__ (or a CNF) when it is _a conjuction of disjunctions of literals_ (a literal being a variable or its negation).

Examples: $X ∨ ¬X$ and $X ∧ (Y ∨ ¬Z)$ are both CNFs, while a formula which is not a CNF is $X ∨ (Y ∧ ¬Z)$.

The disjunctions in a CNF are said to be __clauses__, and a _kCNF_ is a CNF _whose clauses contains at most $k ∈ N$ literals_. Examples: the two formulas $X ∨ ¬X$ and $X ∧ (Y ∨ ¬Z)$ are 2CNFs, but not 1CNFs. (As you can see, there at most 2 elements in the disjunction). 

>[!THEOREM] 
> The following two languages are NP-complete: 
> - $SAT = \{\lfloor F \rfloor | F \text{ is a satisfiable } CNF\}$ 
> - $3SAT = \{\lfloor F \rfloor | F \text{ is a satisfiable } 3CNF\}$

This theorem explains also that the best way of verifying if a sentence is SAT is _using bruteforce_.

The structure of the proof is as follows:
- We consider any language $L ∈ NP$, and we show that $L ≤_p SAT$. 
- To do that, we consider any possible polynomial $p$ and any polytime deterministic TM $M$.

## Proving a problem is hard
Suppose you are studying the complexity of a language $L$, and you are convinced that the underlying problem is hard. How should you back your claim?

- By proving that $L ∈ P$?
	- Of course not, this way you rather prove that $L$ is easy.

- By proving that $L ∈ EXP$?
	- No, the fact that there is an exponential-time algorithm deciding $L$ does not mean that no polynomial-time algorithm for $L$ exist.

- By proving that $L ∈ NP$?
	- Again, this does not mean much.

- ==By proving that $L$ is NP-complete==?
	- Yes, this way you prove that the problem is not so hard (being in $NP$), but not so easy either (unless $P = NP$).

#### Proving a Problem NP-complete
If we want to prove $L$ to be $NP$-complete, we have to prove _two statements_: 
1. __That $L$ is in NP__.
	- This amounts to showing that there are $p$ polynomial and $M$ polytime TM such that $L$ can be written as (_certificate_):
	- ![[certificate.png]]
1. __That any other language $H ∈ NP$ is such that $H ≤_p L$__ (_NP-hardness_)
	- We can of course prove the statement directly. 
	- More often (e.g. when showing 3SAT NP-complete), one rather proves that $J ≤_p L$ for a language $J$ which is already known to be NP-complete. 
	- This is correct, simply because $≤_p$ is _transitive_
	-  ![[Images/reduction.png]]
i.e. to prove that 4SAT is NP, we just use the identity function to reduce it to 3SAT (every CNF that has at most 3 literals, has also at most 4 literals)

### The Zoo of NP-complete Problems
Thousands of different problems are known to be _NP-complete_, such as:
- The __Maximum Independent Set__ Problem `INDSET` is NP-complete. 
	- There is a polytime reduction from `SAT` to `INDSET`. 
- The __Subset Sum__ Problem `SUBSETSUM` is NP-complete. 
	- There are polytime reductions from `3SAT` to a variation `OL3SAT` of it, and from the latter to `SUBSETSUM`. 
- The __Decisional 0/1 Linear Programming__ Problem `ILP` is NP-complete 
	- There is an easy polytime reduction from `SAT` to `ILP`.

For any pair $L, H$ of _NP-complete problems_, we have that:
$$
L \le_p H
$$
$$
H \le_p L
$$
In other words, $L$ and $H$ are __equivalent__.
This being said, defining a reduction from $L$ to $H$ is sometimes easy, and sometimes very difficult, and it is instructive to think at _NP-complete_ problems as forming a graph, where edges are natural polytime reductions.
